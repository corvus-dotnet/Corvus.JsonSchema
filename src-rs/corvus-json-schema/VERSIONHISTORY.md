# Version History

The version history of the `corvus-json-schema` Rust crate. It is versioned independently of the Corvus NuGet packages, whose history is in the repository's [VERSIONHISTORY.md](../../VERSIONHISTORY.md).

## V0.2.0

V0.2.0 matches patterns with the [`corvus-regex-ecma262`](https://crates.io/crates/corvus-regex-ecma262) crate, reads them in the grammar of the dialect, and adds options for the edition and the version of Unicode. It is a minor version because two things that were there change: `CompileOptions` has two new fields, so a struct literal without `..CompileOptions::default()` no longer compiles, and under 2019-09 and 2020-12 a pattern that uses what ECMAScript added after 2020 is no longer valid. The minimum Rust version is 1.89 as before.

### The engine

The crate's own ECMA-262 engine, in `src/ecmaregex`, is gone, and so is the translation of patterns to the [`regex`](https://crates.io/crates/regex) crate. Every pattern that no dedicated matcher takes is matched by `corvus-regex-ecma262` 0.2.5, whose answers are V8's for the whole of the [ECMA-262 regular expression test data](https://github.com/corvus-dotnet/ecma262-regex-test-data). The dedicated matchers (literals, class sequences, `^.*$` and the like) are unchanged. `regex` is still a dependency, for the checks of the `format` keyword.

- **A dedicated matcher only where it is faster.** Each form of pattern with a matcher of its own was timed against the engine, pattern by pattern, over the strings of the jsonschema-benchmark corpora, and kept only where it was the faster. Class sequences, `.*`, `.+`, line lengths, literal prefixes and the lookahead form of cspell keep theirs (0.21 to 0.88 of the engine's time in all). A set of literals keeps its matcher up to four (0.72); ten took 3.2 times the engine's time. Alternatives keep theirs only when there are at most two and neither is anchored at both ends, as in `^([a|A]uto)|([n|N]one)$` (0.22, since the engine searches for the unanchored one); with three, or with an alternative anchored at both ends, the engine was faster by 1.1 to 2.7 times. A separated list (`^([a-z]+)(\.[a-z]+)*$`) was no faster than the engine and has no matcher of its own now. A class sequence without `$` reads only as far as it must: `^[a-z]+` reads one letter, and `^[a-zA-Z0-9_.-]*` matches every string without reading it, where it read the whole string (3.7 times the engine's time). The ignored test `time_corpus_patterns` repeats the measurement.
- **What it costs.** Over the 37 corpora of jsonschema-benchmark, with that benchmark's protocol, 0.2.0 validates in 0.98 of the time of 0.1.7 once warm (geometric mean, five interleaved rounds on one machine), and takes 1.06 of it for the first validation and 1.05 to compile. The engine builds a pattern's automaton on its first match, so the patterns that moved from a dedicated matcher to the engine cost more the first time: jsconfig, whose fifteen case-insensitive alternations moved, takes 2.3 times as long for its first validation (617 µs against 264 µs) and validates in 0.86 of the time once warm.
- **The limits are the engine's.** A match has the limit of time it had (`CompileOptions::pattern_match_limit`), and a pattern the limits of size it had: 1,048,576 bytes, 1,000 levels of groups, and 1,048,576 counted as it is read. `PatternTooLargeError::limit` names the limit as before.
- **The Unicode tables** are the engine's, so the crate no longer carries tables of its own, nor the notice of the Unicode data that went with them.

### The grammar of the dialect

JSON Schema 2019-09 and 2020-12 name the grammar of ECMAScript 2020 (ECMA-262, 11th edition). Under them a `pattern`, a `patternProperties` name and a string checked by the `regex` format are now read with that edition: modifier groups such as `(?i:...)`, a group name used by two groups, and a property or value that is not in the tables of 2020 (such as `\p{Script=Yezidi}`) are not valid with the `u` flag. A modifier group or a shared name is not valid without it either, so a schema with one in a `pattern` is refused. A property escape is valid without the flag, where `\p` is the letter `p`, so such a pattern is read as one with no flag, as a pattern with `\&` is: under 2020-12 `^\p{Script=Yezidi}$` matches the text `p{Script=Yezidi}`. The `regex` format reads only the grammar of the flag, so it refuses all three. Drafts 4 to 7 read today's grammar, as before. A pattern valid in both grammars has the same answers in both.

- **`CompileOptions::regex_edition`** (`Option<RegexEdition>`) chooses the edition for every dialect. `None`, the default, takes the dialect's.
- **`CompileOptions::regex_unicode_version`** (`RegexUnicodeVersion`) chooses the version of Unicode: `V13`, `V17`, `V18`, `Latest`, or `Default` (the default), the version of the V8 the engine's answers are held to, 17 today. It decides the members of each `\p{...}`, the characters of a group name and the case folding of the `i` flag.
- `RegexEdition` and `RegexUnicodeVersion` are the engine's `Edition` and `UnicodeVersion`, re-exported.

## V0.1.7

V0.1.7 replaces the regular expression engine, gives every match of a pattern a limit of time, and bounds what a pattern can cost to compile and to keep. It fixes wrong results for several forms of pattern, a validation that did not end, and a form of pattern that is not valid and was accepted. The functions and types that were there are unchanged. There are new ones for the limits. The minimum Rust version is 1.89 as before. Versions 0.1.0 to 0.1.6 are affected by the bugs.

### The regular expression engine

The patterns that no matcher of the crate's own and not the [`regex`](https://crates.io/crates/regex) crate takes (those with a lookaround, a backreference, `\B`, a modifier group, or a construct `regex` has no counterpart for) were matched by the [`regress`](https://crates.io/crates/regress) crate. They are now matched by the crate's own engine, in `src/ecmaregex`. It is a port of the ECMA-262 engine of the Go module, which the Object Pascal and OCaml ports of the evaluator also carry, with the same files and names so that a fix in one can be carried to the others. `regress` is no longer a dependency. The crate depends on `regex` and `serde_json` alone.

- **Every pattern is matched as it is written.** The first cuts of this version gave `regress` another pattern with the same meaning where its answer was not ECMA-262's, and refused a valid pattern as "valid, but too large to compile" when the pattern written for it came to more than 65,536 characters. There is no such rewriting now and no such refusal. A pattern is refused only when it is not valid, or over a limit of size (below).
- **The grammar** is that of ECMAScript 2025 with the `u` flag: lookbehind, named groups, a name shared by groups in separate alternatives, modifier groups, and every property, value and alias of `\p{...}` that ECMA-262 lists. A pattern that is valid only without the flag (it has `\&` or `\-`, say) is read with the Annex B grammar and matched by UTF-16 code unit over the UTF-8 string, with no UTF-16 copy of the string.
- **Unicode 17, from tables of the crate's own.** The properties and the case folding are the tables of the Go module (`tools/gen-ucd-tables.ps1` writes them from its file, and a test compares the two). Nothing is taken from the Rust standard library or from the tables of the `regex` crate, which is now given a property as its ranges and never by its name. So a pattern matches the same strings whatever toolchain and whatever release of `regex` built the program.
- **How it stays fast.** The engine is a backtracking matcher with its choices on a stack of its own, so a long string cannot overflow the stack of the thread, and a match allocates nothing once that stack has grown. Four things keep it from the time a backtracking matcher can take. A repetition whose body cannot consume a character runs once or not at all. A counted loop gives up where the rest of the pattern needs more text than is left. A way the next character rules out is not taken. And a pattern with no backreference remembers the places from which every way has failed, so a repetition inside a repetition, such as `^(?=.)(a+)+$`, takes time proportional to the pattern times the string. A pattern with no lookaround and no backreference also gets an automaton over ASCII strings, which reads a string once.
- **Which matcher a pattern gets.** The matchers that read a string once and the `regex` crate keep the patterns they had. Of the 137 patterns of the jsonschema-benchmark corpora, 136 have the same matcher and, on `regex`, the same translation as in 0.1.6, and the one that went to `regress` (`^((\.(?!\.)\/)?\w+\/?)+$`) goes to the engine. Four rules are new, and are those of the Go module. A class sequence or a literal that is anchored at neither end, among alternatives, is searched for at every position of a string, so one wider than 64 is left to `regex` or the engine. The last item of a list (`^(item separator)*last$`) must not be able to read past a separator. `regex` is given no program estimated at more than 10,000 instructions (it writes a counted repetition out) and none over 2 MiB (its own default is 10 MiB), so `^[\p{L}\p{N}_-]{1,64}$` goes to the engine, which holds the class once. And `regex` is given a string only when it can answer within the limit of a match (below).

### Limits

A schema and an instance may both come from outside. Neither can now make a validator run without end, exhaust the stack, or hold memory out of proportion to its input.

- **A match has a limit of one second.** `CompileOptions::pattern_match_limit` is the time one match of a `pattern` or `patternProperties` regular expression against one string may take. The default is `DEFAULT_PATTERN_MATCH_LIMIT`, one second. `None`, or a limit of zero, turns it off for a caller who trusts both the schema and the instances. The limit is on each match and not on the validation. The engine counts its steps and reads the clock every 4,096 of them, so a match is abandoned within tens of microseconds of the limit, and a match that ends before its first check never reads the clock. With a limit, the backtracking of a match may hold 1,048,576 entries (24 MB), and its memory of failed places is 8 MB at most.
- **A match that reaches its limit is an error, never an answer.** The new functions `Validator::try_validate`, `try_validate_instance`, `try_validate_json`, `try_evaluate`, `try_evaluate_instance` and `try_evaluate_json` return `Result<bool, SchemaEvaluationError>`. `SchemaEvaluationError` is `InvalidJson`, `DepthExceeded` or `PatternMatchLimit`, and the last holds a `PatternMatchLimitError` with the pattern, the limit and whether it was the memory of the match that ran out. The message names the pattern and the limit. Compiling a schema can report it too (`SchemaCompilationErrorKind::PatternMatchLimit`), because a pattern of `patternProperties` is matched against the property names the schema declares.
- **The functions that were there keep their types.** `is_valid` returns a `bool` and so cannot report an error. It returns `false` for a match that reached its limit, as it does for a schema that recursed beyond the maximum depth, and in neither case is the instance known to be not valid. `validate`, `validate_instance`, `evaluate` and `evaluate_instance` return `Err(SchemaEvaluationDepthError)`, and `validate_json` and `evaluate_json` return `JsonValidationError::DepthExceeded`: their error type has one value for an evaluation that was abandoned, so they say that it was abandoned and not which limit it reached. The message of that error said the schema recursed in place beyond the maximum depth, which is not so at the limit of a match. It now says the validation was abandoned at a limit, names both, and points to the `try_` functions, which tell the two apart and keep a message for each. No type and no signature changes. `is_valid` fails closed: a limit is `false`, never `true`.
- **A pattern has limits of size.** A pattern is refused when it is longer than 1,048,576 bytes, when its groups nest more than 1,000 deep, or when its size as it is read is over 1,048,576 (64 for each term, 32 for each alternative, 8 for each range of a class, and what a case-insensitive group adds). A pattern at the limit compiles in milliseconds and keeps a few megabytes. An ordinary character is one term, so the longest pattern of plain characters that compiles is 16,384 characters, where 0.1.6 compiled longer ones. The schema then fails to compile with a message that says the pattern is too large, and not that it is not valid: the pattern was not read to its end. `SchemaCompilationError::kind` tells the cases apart (`PatternTooLarge` with a `PatternTooLargeError` that has the pattern, the keyword, the name of the limit and its value, `InvalidPattern`, `PatternMatchLimit`, and `Other`). The `regex` format answers `false` for a string over a limit. The sizes are counted as the Go, Object Pascal and OCaml ports count them (a test compares 122 patterns), so a schema one port compiles is not refused by another.
- **The cache of compiled patterns is bounded.** Identical patterns share one matcher through a cache of the process, which was never emptied. It now holds 1,024 patterns at most and 64 MiB at most in the bytes counted for them (the source, four times the size above, and for a pattern on `regex` an estimate of its program). Over either bound the oldest go. A validator holds the patterns of its own schema, so nothing it uses is lost.
- **A long string is not an internationalized host name.** The checks of the `format` keyword read strings of the instance, and none is under the limit of a match. The check of `idn-hostname`, which is also the check of the domain of an `idn-email`, decodes and encodes Punycode and walks the neighbours of every joiner, which takes time that grows with the square of a label, and it did so for a label of any length: a host name of 100,000 different letters took 4 seconds to refuse, and one of a million took 41. An internationalized host name has 253 characters at most, so a string over 1,012 bytes is now refused without being read. No answer changes. Every other format was measured on 248 strings of 100,000 and of 1,000,000 characters made to be slow, and takes time proportional to the string: the slowest of them took 12 ms for a million characters.
- **What is not bounded.** The limit is for each match, so a validation of many strings takes the sum of its matches. The matchers that read a string once, and `regex`, are not stopped: they take time proportional to the string, and `regex` is given a string only when its program times the string, at 16 ns for each instruction and byte, is within the limit (the most measured was 3.6 ns, with its lazy automaton defeated). A string beyond that goes to the engine, which can be stopped. With the limit turned off nothing bounds a match.

### Bug fixes

- **A separated list with a `$` inside it.** The crate matches a pattern of the form `^item(separator item)*$`, such as `^([a-z]+)(\.[a-z]+)*$`, without a regular expression engine (and likewise the forms with `+`, and `^(item separator)*item$`). It read the first item and the repeated group as sequences of characters and classes, and a `$` at the end of either was read and then dropped. So `^x(,a$)*$` was matched as `^x(,a)*$`. A `$` holds only at the end of the string, so the group of `^x(,a$)*$` can match once at most, and only as the last thing in the string. `{"pattern": "^x(,a$)*$"}` was valid for `"x,a,a"`, where it is not. `^x$(,a)*$` was valid for `"x,a"`, and `^(a,$)*b$` for `"a,b"`. A pattern used for `patternProperties` or `propertyNames` was affected in the same way. A list with a `$` at the end of its first item or of its group is now matched by a regular expression engine. An escaped `\$` is a literal character and such a list is matched as before. No other form of pattern was affected. A table of 101 patterns with an anchor, a word boundary or a lookaround inside one of the forms the crate matches by itself, with answers from V8, is now part of the tests (`tests/pattern_anchors.rs`).

The fixes below are for the patterns that went to `regress`, which answers some of them differently from ECMA-262. The engine that has them now answers each as ECMA-262 does.

- **A repetition inside a repetition that did not end, or lost its count.** `regress` 0.12 sets the iteration count of a repetition to zero each time the repetition starts, and going back by backtracking does not put the old count back. With a body that can match the empty string the match then went round for ever. `{"pattern": "^(?!-)(?:(?:a*)*)*x"}` on `"a"` took memory until the process was stopped, and no instance was needed beyond that one string. With other bodies the count was wrong. `{"pattern": "^(?!-)(?:(?:(?:a){1,}){1}){3}$"}` was not valid for `"aaa"`, where it is, and `{"pattern": "^(?!-)(?:(?:a|ax){0,300}){2}$"}` was valid for `ax` followed by 600 times `a`, which is 601 iterations for two repetitions of 300.

- **A repetition inside a repetition that took time doubling with each character.** `regress` backtracks with nothing to stop it going through one place twice, so a pattern such as `^((\.(?!\.)\/)?\w+\/?)+$` (it is in the ui5-manifest schema of the jsonschema-benchmark corpora) took twice as long for each character of a string that does not match. `{"pattern": "^((\\.(?!\\.)\\/)?\\w+\\/?)+$"}` on the 47 characters of `"sap/ui/core/mvc/Controller/extension/Component!"` was stopped after 17 minutes without an answer. The engine remembers the places from which every way has failed, and answers it at once. Where a pattern has a backreference there is nothing it can remember, and the limit of a match ends what would not end.

- **A repetition of a body that can only match the empty string.** A body of assertions and lookarounds, as in `(?:^|\b|(?=a)){4,}`, matches at one position however often it is repeated. `regress` tried every way through every iteration, which for a few such repetitions inside each other took minutes. The body is matched once, or not at all for a minimum of zero, which is what ECMA-262's rule for empty iterations comes to.

- **A backreference to a group from inside the group.** A group has no value until it closes, so `\1` inside group 1 matches the empty string. `regress` read the value the group had in an alternative it had backtracked out of. `{"pattern": "(x|\\1)x"}` was not valid for `"x"`, where it is.

- **Two groups with one name.** ECMAScript 2025 allows it for groups in separate alternatives. A reference to the name matched the empty string whichever group had matched, so `{"pattern": "^(?:(?<n>a)|(?<n>x))\\k<n>$"}` was valid for `"a"`, where only `"aa"` and `"xx"` are.

- **A quantifier on a word boundary.** `\b+`, `\B*` and `\b{2}` are not valid in ECMA-262, with or without the `u` flag. They were accepted. A schema with such a pattern now fails to compile, and the `regex` format refuses it.

- **A case-insensitive group.** In a pattern that is valid only without the `u` flag, such as one with `\&` or `\-`, two characters are equal in `(?i:...)` when the uppercase forms of their UTF-16 code units are. `regress` compared by Unicode case folding as it does with the flag, so `{"pattern": "^(?i:s)\\&?$"}` was valid for `"\u017f"` (the long s), and `^(?i:k)\&?$` for the Kelvin sign. With the `u` flag, `\W` in a class of such a group, as in `(?i:[\W])`, held the two characters that fold to a word character and so matched `s` and `k`.

- **A case-insensitive backreference.** A backreference inside `(?i:...)`, in a pattern that is valid only without the `u` flag, was compared by `regress` with a folding of its own, which equates every pair ECMA-262 does and 31 more: `i` and `I` with U+0131, `s` and `S` with U+017F, and each of the 27 Greek letters with a iota below it with its capital form. So `{"pattern": "^(?i:(\u017f)\\1)\\&?$"}` was valid for the long s followed by `S`.

- **A pattern that is valid only without the `u` flag.** ECMA-262 matches such a pattern by UTF-16 code unit, and a character beyond the Basic Multilingual Plane is two of them. The crate matched it by character. `{"pattern": "^\\-?.$"}` was valid for `"\ud801\udc00"`, where it is not: the dot takes one code unit. A quantifier after such a character in the pattern repeats its second code unit, a class holds each of the two, and a match can start or end between them.

- **`\B` beside a character of several bytes.** A pattern with `\B` that the `regex` crate could run was translated for it, with `\B` as `(?-u:\B)`. That holds between the bytes of a character too, and `regex` 1.13.1 then reports no match where there is one. `{"pattern": "s*?\\B|.{2}.$"}` was not valid for `"k\u017fk"`, where it is. A pattern with `\B` now goes to the engine. `\b` was not affected.

- **A negated class with the characters on both sides of the surrogates.** `regex` 1.13.1 negates a class wrongly when one of its ranges ends at U+D7FF and another starts at U+E000: the complement takes both characters in. `{"pattern": "^[^\\ud7ff\\ue000]$"}` was valid for `"\ud7ff"` and for `"\ue000"`, where it is not. Such a class now goes to the engine.

- **The escape of a lone surrogate.** With the `u` flag `\ud801` on its own is valid and matches nothing in a string of characters. `regress` lost a quantifier after it, so `^\ud801*$` did not match the empty string.

- **`\k` in a class.** In a pattern with a named group `\k` is not valid in a class, with or without the `u` flag. `regress` reads it as the letter there. `{"pattern": "(?<n>a)[\\k]"}` was accepted, and now fails to compile. In a pattern with no named group `[\k]` is still the letter.

- **A count with its numbers out of order.** `a{3,2}` is not valid. `regress` reads a number of more than ten digits as the largest it holds, so `a{99999999999999999999,99999999999999999998}` was accepted. The two numbers are compared as they are written. V8 accepts that pattern and `a{2147483648,2147483647}` too. ECMA-262 says a first number greater than the second is a syntax error, and the crate follows it.

- **The Script value `Hrkt`.** `\p{Script=Hrkt}`, `\p{sc=Katakana_Or_Hiragana}` and the same for `Script_Extensions` are valid with the `u` flag: the value is in Unicode's list of property value aliases, which is what ECMA-262 takes. No character has it, so the escape matches nothing and its negation matches every character. `regress` does not know the value, as V8 13.6 does not, and the pattern was read without the flag, as the letter `p` and the text in braces. `{"pattern": "^\\P{sc=Hrkt}$"}` was not valid for `"a"`, where it is.

- **A pattern nested more than 256 groups deep was refused as not valid.** `regress` reads 256 levels of groups, so a valid pattern nested deeper, such as 300 groups one inside another, failed to compile with the message of a pattern that is not valid. It compiles now, up to the limit of 1,000 levels, and beyond that the message says it is too large. The engine reads a pattern that opens more than 32 groups on a thread with a stack of its own, since a thousand levels do not fit the stack a thread can be relied on to have.

- **A very long pattern could end the process or take seconds to refuse.** A pattern of a million characters of alternatives overflowed the stack, which ends the process, and one of 100,000 groups took 12 seconds to be refused. Both are over the limit of size now and are refused in a hundredth of a second.

### What was measured

- **The ECMA-262 test data.** The crate gives V8's answer for every pattern and text of the [ECMA-262 regular expression test data](https://github.com/corvus-dotnet/ecma262-regex-test-data) that it can be asked: 98,839 patterns in thirteen files, of which 25,643 are not valid, and 4,268,313 texts. No answer is wrong, no pattern is refused or accepted wrongly, and no match reaches the limit of one second. Two kinds of text are not asked. The 46,500 texts with half of a surrogate pair cannot be held by a Rust string, and a JSON document with the escape of one is refused as not valid JSON. The texts of the 62 records where V8 itself departs from ECMA-262 are not compared, and the crate follows ECMA-262 there: V8 starts a match between the two halves of a surrogate pair with the `u` flag, compares a case-insensitive backreference to a group of several characters by full case folding, and answers some case-insensitive modifier groups differently from the same pattern with the `i` flag.
- **The same answers as the Go engine.** On 478,804 records in 64 files (the test data, generated sets of property names and Annex B syntax, and fuzz sets), the crate and the Go module give the same status and the same answer for every text of every record. Against V8 both differ on the same 2,582 texts in 306 patterns, each of them one of V8's departures above or one of the four scripts Unicode 17 adds, which V8 13.6 does not know. One record has three texts that reach the limit of one second, in both.
- **An adversarial probe.** 167 patterns built to be slow or large (nested repetitions that can match nothing, backreferences inside loops, counts such as `(?:a{1000}){1000}`, ten thousand open parentheses, long alternations, lookbehinds over long text, case-insensitive references to wide groups, classes of properties, and the shapes the crate matches by itself), each on a text of up to 100,000 characters in a process of its own. With the engine this version started from, 21 did not answer in the 20 seconds they had, one ended the process with a stack overflow, one was refused only after 12 seconds, and the most memory a process took was 5,138 MB. Now every one ends in an answer (132), the error of the size (14) or the error of the limit (21), the slowest in 1.01 seconds, and the most memory a process takes is 28 MB. Each ends as it does in the Go module.

### Known limits

- **A search no engine finishes ends at the limit.** `(?:a|){100000}` inside a repetition is a hundred thousand iterations that may each match nothing, which V8 does not finish either. The first cuts of this version wrote that shape as `a{0,100000}` for `regress` and so answered it. The engine matches it as written, and where it does not come to an answer the match ends at its limit with the error. The same holds for a group read back inside a repetition, such as `^(?:(a+)\1*)+$` on sixty `a` and a `b`, where what the group holds changes what follows and nothing can be remembered.
- **The functions that were there do not say which limit was reached** (above). Changing their error types would break the code that names them, so that is left for a version that can.
- **Lone surrogates.** A JSON string with the escape of half of a surrogate pair (`"\ud800"`) is not a Rust string. `validate_json` and `serde_json` both refuse the document as not valid JSON.
- **Unicode versions.** The tables are Unicode 17. V8 13.6 has Unicode 16. They agree on every character of Unicode 16, and the four scripts Unicode 17 adds (`Sidetic`, `Tolong_Siki`, `Beria_Erfe`, `Tai_Yo`) are scripts here.

### Notices

- **The notice of the Unicode data is in the crate.** The Unicode tables of the crate's ECMA-262 engine are derived from the Unicode Character Database, which is under the Unicode License V3. That license asks that its notice goes with every copy of the data. `THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES.txt` has it, with the notice of the `regress` crate that the tables were first read from, and the crate's package on crates.io now holds it. Versions 0.1.0 to 0.1.6 carried no such notice.

## V0.1.6

V0.1.6 fixes a stack overflow, and a wrong `is_valid` result, for a schema whose `not` is part of a loop. There are no API changes. Versions 0.1.0 to 0.1.5 are affected.

### Bug fixes

- **A `not` that leads back to the schema it is in.** A schema can loop without consuming the instance. The crate abandons such an evaluation at `CompileOptions::max_depth` (128 by default) and reports `SchemaEvaluationDepthError`. Evaluating `not` went around that guard. For a schema such as `{"not": {"$ref": "#"}}`, validating an instance that reached the `not` recursed until the stack overflowed, and a stack overflow aborts the process. Every function was affected (`is_valid`, `validate`, `validate_json`, `validate_instance` and the three `evaluate` functions). A `not` is now under the guard, so these functions return the depth error and `is_valid` returns false. The fault is in the schema. No instance causes it for a schema without such a loop, so a program was exposed only if it compiled schemas it did not write.

- **`is_valid` for a schema that loops under a `not`.** With the loop elsewhere, as in `{"$defs": {"loop": {"allOf": [{"$ref": "#/$defs/loop"}]}}, "not": {"$ref": "#/$defs/loop"}}`, the guard stopped the loop and counted the abandoned branch as false, and the `not` turned that into true. `is_valid` returned true for an evaluation that had been abandoned, where its documentation says false. It now returns false whenever the evaluation went beyond the maximum depth, whatever the rest of the schema came to. The same rule changes `is_valid` for a schema whose `anyOf` recovers after the depth is exceeded, such as `{"$defs": {"a": {"anyOf": [{"$ref": "#/$defs/a"}, true]}}, "$ref": "#/$defs/a"}`. Its first branch loops and is abandoned, and its second is true. `is_valid` returned true for it and now returns false, as its documentation says. The functions that return a `Result` reported the depth error for both schemas, and still do.

## V0.1.5

V0.1.5 corrects the minimum Rust version the crate declares. There are no code or API changes.

### Bug fixes

- **The crate declares Rust 1.89 as its minimum (`rust-version`), not 1.85.** Versions 0.1.0 to 0.1.4 declared 1.85 but do not build with it: the `regress` dependency (0.12) uses `let` chains, which need Rust 1.88, and the crate's own source needs 1.89. With the declared version, Cargo chose these versions for a 1.85 toolchain and the build then failed in `regress` with "`let` expressions in this position are unstable". The crate's CI now builds it with the version it declares.

## V0.1.4

V0.1.4 fixes wrong validation results for one form of pattern. There are no API changes. Versions 0.1.0 to 0.1.3 are affected.

### Bug fixes

- **A pattern of the form `^(?=[^SET]+$)(?=(.*\w)).+$` with a character outside ASCII in its excluded set.** The crate matches this form without the regular expression engine and keeps the excluded set as one bit for each ASCII character. A member outside ASCII, such as `é` in `^(?=[^é]+$)(?=(.*\w)).+$`, was read one UTF-8 byte at a time as a bit number of 128 or more. In a release build the shift wrapped, so the set held unrelated ASCII characters (`C` and `)` for `é`) and not the member. The pattern then rejected valid strings (`"C1"`) and accepted invalid ones (`"é1"`). In a debug build, compiling the schema panicked with a shift overflow. Such a pattern is now matched by the regular expression engine, with the results ECMA-262 gives. A pattern of this form whose excluded set is all ASCII was never affected, and is still matched without the engine.

## V0.1.3

V0.1.3 validates JSON text without allocating, and reads a number in a schema and the same number in an instance as the same double. There are no breaking changes.

### New features

- **`Validator::validate_json` and `Validator::evaluate_json`.** They take JSON text, parse it into the `JsonDocument` parser's per-thread buffers and evaluate it in place: in the steady state a validation of JSON text allocates nothing (a test counts allocations). `JsonValidationError` tells invalid JSON (with its byte offset) from a schema that recurses in place beyond the maximum depth. A format validator that itself validates JSON text, during a validation of JSON text, gets buffers of its own. Use `JsonDocument` to validate the same text more than once.

### Bug fixes

- **Numbers in schemas and instances are read alike.** The crate turns on serde_json's `float_roundtrip` feature. Without it, serde_json read some numbers one unit in the last place off the correctly rounded double, including its own output (`9.727837981879871e+26`), while `JsonDocument` rounds correctly. A schema passed as JSON text and an instance validated as JSON text could then hold two different doubles for one literal, so an `exclusiveMaximum` equal to the instance passed. The JSON-Schema-Test-Suite's `bignum.json` found it, run through the C library.

## V0.1.2

V0.1.2 adds `JsonDocument`, a parsed form of JSON text that the evaluator reads in place, and makes validation faster on schemas where the Blaze C++ validator was faster. There are no breaking changes, and every result is unchanged.

### New features

- **`JsonDocument` parses JSON text for evaluation.** `JsonDocument::parse` reads JSON text into one flat array of values, with strings borrowed from the text where they have no escapes, and `JsonDocument::root` is an `Instance` for `Validator::validate_instance` and `Validator::evaluate_instance`. Parsing takes about a quarter of the time of parsing into a `serde_json::Value`, and a document costs one allocation (two when strings need unescaping), against one or more per string, array and object for a `Value`. Validation reads it at least as fast as a `Value`. It accepts what serde_json accepts: nesting up to 127 levels, no lone surrogates in `\u` escapes, numbers within the range of a double; of duplicate property names, the last value is kept at the position of the first. Doubles are correctly rounded, where serde_json's parser (without its `float_roundtrip` feature) can be one unit in the last place off. `JsonDocument::to_value` converts a document to a `serde_json::Value`.

### Other changes

- **Faster validation.** On the sourcemeta jsonschema-benchmark corpora, measured with the benchmark's own harness against Blaze:
  - The `\w` test of the matcher for patterns such as `^(?=[^!*,;{}[\]~\n]+$)(?=(.*\w)).+$` no longer rebuilds its character set for every character: cspell validates more than five times faster.
  - A pattern that is valid only without the `u` flag (an identity escape such as `\&`) runs on the `regex` crate for strings within the Basic Multilingual Plane, instead of always on the backtracking engine: krakend validates about 30% faster.
  - Small objects cost less to evaluate: a plan's shape is entered directly, a few declared names are looked up in a small object instead of matching each of its properties, a property name that is not the next declared one is tried as the next in sorted order, and fused object plans no longer allocate per evaluation.

  With a `JsonDocument`, the crate now parses and validates faster than Blaze on all 37 corpora (geometric mean about 0.37 of Blaze's time), and validates already-parsed instances faster on 34 of them (about 0.54).

## V0.1.1

V0.1.1 makes the evaluator generic over the instance it reads.

### New features

- **The `Instance` trait.** `Validator::validate_instance` and `Validator::evaluate_instance` take any `Instance`: a value shown as one of the six JSON kinds, with arrays and objects read in place through `ArrayView` and `ObjectView`. `&serde_json::Value` implements it, and so can a host language's own values, which can then be validated without converting them to `serde_json::Value` first. The `corvus-json-schema-rs` Python package reads Python objects this way.

## V0.1.0

The first release: a port of the Corvus.Text.Json V5 runtime evaluator (`Corvus.Text.Json.RuntimeEvaluator`) to Rust, for drafts 4, 6, 7, 2019-09 and 2020-12. It passes the whole JSON-Schema-Test-Suite and produces the same results and annotations as the C# evaluator.
